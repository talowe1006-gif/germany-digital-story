Global Data Story Project · Germany

Germany in the Digital Age

How data and technology shape society, business and privacy in Europe's largest economy.

01 / Country overview

A country balancing innovation and privacy

Germany is located in Central Europe, with Berlin as its capital. It has approximately 84 million residents and the largest economy in the European Union. Its economy is known for automotive manufacturing, engineering, chemicals and technology. Companies such as BMW, Volkswagen, Siemens and SAP demonstrate the importance of technology to German industry.

I selected Germany because I wanted to understand how a country with a strong manufacturing economy uses technology while protecting personal information. The central question of this project is how Germany can benefit from digital innovation without leaving people behind or sacrificing privacy.

02 / Technology landscape

From factories to everyday life

Industry 4.0

Connected machines, sensors, robotics and data analysis allow manufacturers to monitor production and improve efficiency.

Digital participation

Internet access supports education, communication, shopping and banking. Digital skills determine how easily people can benefit.

Privacy by design

The EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) gives individuals rights over personal data and establishes responsibilities for organizations.

03 / Visualization one

Internet use is becoming nearly universal

Internet usage increased by four percentage points over the period shown. This suggests that online services are becoming more accessible and important in everyday life. However, widespread internet use does not necessarily mean everyone has the same connection quality or digital skills. Germany must continue supporting citizens who struggle to access digital services.

04 / Visualization two

Artificial intelligence is changing business

AI adoption more than doubled between 2021 and 2025 in the figures shown. Businesses can use AI to automate tasks, analyze information and improve production. This is especially relevant to Germany's manufacturing economy. However, AI also raises concerns about employee privacy, job security and accountability for automated decisions.

05 / Visualization three

The digital economy is not equal for everyone

The difference between the 25–44 and 65–74 age groups is 32 percentage points. Older citizens may have different shopping preferences, security concerns or levels of digital experience. The chart does not establish which explanation is correct. As services move online, accessible designs, digital education and offline alternatives can help prevent exclusion.

06 / Cultural context

Why privacy matters in Germany

Germany's experience with surveillance under Nazi rule and the East German Stasi helps explain the importance of privacy in its public debate. Historical experiences have shaped concerns about the misuse of personal information, although citizens do not all share the same views.

The GDPR, applicable since 2018, establishes rights such as access to personal information and, under certain conditions, its deletion. It also requires organizations to have a lawful basis for processing personal data. These protections matter as businesses increasingly rely on AI and data analytics.

Germany also faces challenges related to age, income and rural connectivity. Innovation is most valuable when people can participate regardless of their background or technological experience.

07 / Global connections

What other countries can learn

Germany and the United States both use technology to support economic growth, but their privacy frameworks differ. Germany follows the EU's comprehensive GDPR framework, while the United States relies on a combination of federal sector-specific and state privacy laws.

Germany's manufacturers participate in international supply chains, and their digital systems depend on global technology providers. Cross-border data transfers therefore require cooperation and safeguards. Other countries can learn from Germany's efforts to combine industrial innovation with individual rights, while recognizing that regulation and digital inclusion remain ongoing challenges.

08 / Personal reflection

What I learned

One thing that surprised me was how Germany can be a leader in manufacturing and still place such a strong emphasis on privacy. Before researching this topic, I assumed that developed countries approached technology in similar ways. I now understand how history and culture can influence technology policy.

The differences between age groups also showed me that having access to technology does not mean everyone uses it in the same way. Comparing statistics requires paying attention to the population being measured and the time period. Overall, this project helped me understand that technology is not only about efficiency, but also about fairness, privacy and opportunity.
